# Literary Review: *Święty czas, święta ziemia* (Holy Time, Holy Ground)

## Executive Summary

*Święty czas, święta ziemia* is a first-person narrative set in a pre-Christian Celtic-influenced village, told through the eyes of Jonas, a young gravedigger who inherits the role of Guardian of the Dead after his father is killed during a sudden attack by mythological beasts. The story opens on a festival day, weaving together Jonas's budding romance with Mirella—an apprentice healer undergoing initiation into the cult of Danu—and his fraught relationship with a community that fears him. What begins as a tender pastoral quickly transforms into brutal violence, psychological trauma, and a devastating meditation on duty, grief, and the boundary between the living and the dead. At its core, the story asks: what happens when a man defined by death is offered life—and has it torn away before he can grasp it?

---

## Plot & Pacing

The narrative architecture of this story is masterful in its use of rhythm and rupture. The first act unfolds with deliberate, sensory slowness: Jonas tends his black roses, converses with his sister Jana, and recalls intimate moments with Mirella. The prose lingers on texture—soil, moisture, thorns—establishing a world felt through the body rather than merely observed.

The second act pivots around the festival night and Mirella's revelation at the Nemeton. Here the pacing accelerates with breathless momentum. The sacred-erotic encounter is handled with remarkable restraint—suggested rather than described—before the return journey shatters the idyll with the appearance of the dying Tarwos.

The third act is where the story achieves something extraordinary. The attack's aftermath is rendered in a radically fragmented style: short, declarative sentences stripped of emotion, disconnected vignettes separated by asterisks. This is not a pacing choice—it is a *psychological* one. The narrative itself dissociates alongside its narrator, mimicking the clinical detachment of trauma response. Time collapses. Dialogue vanishes. Only action remains: *Pcham wózek przed sobą* ("I push the cart before me") becomes a haunting refrain.

The climax—Jonas digging his father's grave—restores temporal continuity and emotional depth simultaneously. The struggle with the buried boulder serves as both physical ordeal and psychological catharsis, culminating in the devastating irony of the final line: the rammer breaks, exactly as his father predicted.

The resolution is withheld. The story ends on the broken tool—a perfect image of interrupted grief, unfinished work, and the cruel accuracy of a dead man's last words.

## Character Analysis

### Jonas
Jonas is a profoundly physical character whose inner world is accessed almost exclusively through sensation. He knows the world through soil temperature, thorn pricks, the weight of bodies. He is not inarticulate—his narration is rich and precise—but he is emotionally muted, conditioned by isolation and his father's stoic philosophy (*"These are matters of the living; we serve the dead"*). His arc traces a single devastating day in which he experiences, for the first time, genuine intimacy, communal violence, and total loss in rapid succession. The dissociative episode that follows the attack is rendered with clinical precision—Jonas becomes an automaton, loading bodies, pushing his cart, unable to hear or respond to the living. He is dynamic in the truest sense: not through speech or decision, but through the gradual cracking of his emotional armor, which only fully breaks in the grave-digging scene.

### Mirella
Mirella functions as Jonas's opposite and complement. Where he is rooted and silent, she is mobile and expressive—literally dancing with joy at her initiation. She bridges worlds: healer, herbalist, keeper of forbidden knowledge, yet also pragmatic and warm. Her choice of Jonas for the fertility ritual reveals sophisticated emotional intelligence; she uses sacred obligation as a vehicle for genuine desire. Her brief appearances after the attack—a hand on his chest, tears, a whispered promise to care for Jana—demonstrate her capacity to reach him even in his deepest withdrawal.

### Jakob (the father)
Though present only briefly in real-time, Jakob dominates the story's emotional architecture. His obsessive tool-sharpening, his terse commands, his uncanny premonitions—all paint a portrait of a man who has compressed his entire emotional life into craft and duty. His final act—releasing Jonas to the festival, then dying in defense of the village—transforms him from apparent antagonist to tragic hero. The discovery of his body, rydel still in hand, is the story's emotional nadir.

### Jana
Jana serves as the story's connective tissue to ordinary social life. Her cheerfulness, her matchmaking, her screams upon discovering her father—she represents everything Jonas cannot access: easy emotion, community, normalcy.

## Themes & Symbolism

### The Boundary Between Life and Death
The central thematic axis. Jonas literally lives on the border—at the cemetery's edge, between village and wilderness. The phrase *"To są sprawy żywych, my służymy umarłym"* ("These are matters of the living; we serve the dead") is both professional creed and existential prison. The story systematically dismantles this boundary: the fertility ritual (life from sacred ground), the attack (death invading life), the dissociative episode (Jonas becoming death-like while alive), and the grave-digging (entering the earth himself).

### Earth and Soil
The dominant symbol. Earth is workspace, lover's bed, grave, and identity. Jonas's expertise in soil layers—the black topsoil, the grey clay, the glacial boulder—maps directly onto psychological depth. The story's title (*Holy Time, Holy Ground*) consecrates this element as sacred. The act of digging becomes simultaneously burial and birth—Jonas enters the earth and emerges transformed.

### The Black Roses
A brilliant recurring motif. Their disease (*czerń*—"blackness") requires surgical intervention: cutting away infected parts, burning them completely, ensuring no moisture enables spread. This mirrors the community's treatment of Jonas himself—something beautiful but dangerous, associated with death, requiring careful management. His tender care for them reveals his capacity for nurture, invisible to all but himself.

### The Cart (*wózek*)
The cart is Jonas's professional tool and his emotional crutch. During the dissociative episode, pushing it becomes compulsive ritual—the one action that still makes sense when meaning has collapsed. Its repetition (*Pcham wózek przed sobą*) creates an almost liturgical rhythm.

### The Rammer (*ubijak*)
The story's final symbol. Jakob's insistence on its repair—and Jonas's dismissal—sets up the devastating punchline. When it breaks at the story's end, it carries multiple meanings: the father's prescience validated too late; the inadequacy of quick fixes for deep damage; the impossibility of completing grief-work with broken tools; and the suggestion that Jonas himself is the broken instrument, repaired insufficiently for the task ahead.

### Sacred vs. Profane Order
The tension between the cult of Danu (Celtic, feminine, earth-based, secret) and the established order (Romanized names, Father Baltazar, institutional authority) permeates the world. Jonas serves the new order; Mirella serves the old. Their union—on holy ground, in holy time—is itself a transgressive synthesis.

## Writing Style & Tone

The prose operates in two distinct registers that create the story's devastating emotional range.

**The sensory mode** dominates the first half: long, flowing sentences saturated with physical detail. The author writes earth the way a sommelier writes wine—with granular specificity about moisture, texture, temperature, and smell. This is not decorative; it establishes Jonas's consciousness as fundamentally embodied. The Polish language serves this beautifully, its inflected structure allowing dense, rhythmic descriptions that feel almost incantatory.

**The traumatic mode** in the second half strips everything away. Sentences shorten to three or four words. Verbs become bare infinitives or present tense. Sensory richness vanishes, replaced by flat enumeration. The contrast is visceral—the reader experiences the dissociation alongside Jonas because the *prose itself* dissociates.

The first-person perspective is perfectly chosen. Jonas's limited emotional vocabulary makes his moments of feeling—the cold water's shock, Mirella's warmth, the rage against the boulder—land with extraordinary force precisely because they are rare.

The epigraph from Ady Endre (*"Szeretem a beteg rózsákat"*—"I love the sick roses") elegantly prefigures the story's central concerns: beauty in decay, tenderness toward the dying, the kinship with death that defines Jonas's existence.

## Strengths & Weaknesses

### Strengths

1. **Formal innovation through trauma representation.** The shift from lyrical to dissociative prose is not merely stylistic—it is the story's argument made visible. Few writers achieve this level of integration between form and content.

2. **Physical specificity.** The author clearly possesses (or has meticulously researched) deep knowledge of gravedigging, horticulture, soil science, and rural craft. This grounds the fantasy elements in irrefutable material reality.

3. **Emotional restraint.** The story earns its devastating moments by *withholding* rather than indulging. The sex scene is elided. The father's death is discovered, not witnessed. Jonas's grief emerges through a battle with a boulder, not through tears or monologue.

4. **Structural irony.** The father's prophecy about the rammer, dismissed as mere obstinacy, becomes the story's final word—a perfect encapsulation of everything Jonas failed to understand about his father until too late.

5. **Mythological integration.** The Tarwos, Garany, Nemeton, and cult of Danu are woven organically into a world that feels lived-in rather than constructed. The supernatural is matter-of-fact, which makes it terrifying.

### Weaknesses

1. **Density of the opening.** The first section—rose-tending, the memory of Mirella and the larvae—requires patience. Readers unfamiliar with the slow-cinema aesthetic may find the extended horticultural detail excessive before the story's stakes become clear.

2. **Supporting cast opacity.** Characters like Peire, Falko, Arnaut, and Raimon appear only as corpses. While this is arguably the point—Jonas encounters the dead, not the living—it limits the community's emotional weight. We grieve for Jonas's loss, but the village itself remains somewhat abstract.

3. **The Nemeton sequence's tonal shift.** Mirella's giddiness—jumping, giggling, throwing her wreath—contrasts sharply with the otherwise somber register. While this characterizes her vitality, it risks reading as tonally inconsistent, particularly given the gravity of what she proposes.

4. **Accessibility.** The story's Polish, while beautiful, is demanding—long sentences, archaic vocabulary, specialized terminology. Combined with the Celtic mythological framework, this creates a high barrier to entry for casual readers.

---

## Final Verdict

*Święty czas, święta ziemia* is a remarkable piece of literary fiction that operates simultaneously as intimate character study, mythological horror, grief narrative, and formal experiment. Its greatest achievement is the seamless integration of psychological realism with folkloric fantasy—a world where soil science and sacred ritual coexist without contradiction.

This story is for readers who appreciate slow-burning literary fiction with sudden violence (*Blood Meridian*, *Under the Skin*); for those drawn to mythological realism in the tradition of Olga Tokarczuk or Naomi Novik's Slavic works; and for anyone who believes that how a story is told can be as meaningful as what it tells.

It is not for readers seeking fast plot, extensive dialogue, or emotional accessibility. Jonas's interiority is hard-won and deliberately opaque. The reward lies in the accumulation of physical detail that, by the story's end, achieves the weight of genuine grief.

The broken rammer in the final line is a stroke of genius—an ending that is simultaneously anticlimactic and devastating, comic and tragic, mundane and symbolic. It leaves the reader exactly where Jonas stands: with unfinished work, inadequate tools, and the terrible knowledge that the dead were right all along.

**Rating: ★★★★½** — A formally ambitious, emotionally devastating debut that announces a significant literary voice. Its demands on the reader are considerable, but the rewards are proportionate.
